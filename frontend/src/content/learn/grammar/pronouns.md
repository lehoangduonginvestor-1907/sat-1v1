Pronoun questions give you choices like *it / they / its / their* or *this / these / that / those*. The test is almost always the same: **what noun does the pronoun refer to, and is that noun singular or plural?**

## Find the antecedent

The noun a pronoun replaces is its **antecedent**. Usually it comes earlier in the same sentence, but it can be in the previous sentence.

:::steps
1. Notice the choices mix singular and plural pronouns.
2. Ask: what is doing this action, or owns this thing? Find that noun in the text.
3. Decide whether that noun is singular or plural, then pick the matching pronoun.
:::

:::examples
✓ The **museum** reopened last spring, and **its** new wing has already drawn record crowds. // One museum → its.
✗ The museum reopened last spring, and their new wing has already drawn record crowds.
✓ **Honeybees** communicate by dancing; **they** use the angle of the dance to show direction. // Plural → they.
:::

As with verbs, the nearest noun is often a decoy: "The **company** that built the region's first wind **turbines** later moved **its** headquarters overseas." The plural "turbines" sits right before the blank, but turbines don't have headquarters; the company does, so the pronoun is the singular *its*. Always reason from meaning.

## The pronoun table

| | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| things | it, its, itself | they, them, their, themselves |
| people | he, she, him, her, his, hers | they, them, their |
| pointing | this, that | these, those |
| possessive (no apostrophe!) | its, his, her, whose | their, whose |

*Its* is **never** plural, and the SAT treats collective nouns (*company, team, government, committee*) as singular: "The company announced **its** results."

## Clarity: no vague or missing antecedents

A pronoun must point clearly to a noun that actually appears in the text.

- If two nouns could both be the antecedent, the SAT prefers the choice that **names the noun** instead of using a pronoun: ~~"When the architect met the mayor, she approved the design"~~ → "…, **the mayor** approved the design."
- A pronoun can't refer to a noun that's only implied. ~~"At the observatory, they let visitors use the telescope"~~ has no "they." → "The **observatory staff** let…"
- *This*, *that*, *these*, and *those* are clearer with a noun after them: "**This approach** reduced costs," not just "This reduced costs."

## Consistency: one, you, we

If a passage uses *one* ("One can see the coast from the summit"), keep using *one*, not *you*. If it uses *we*, keep *we*. Don't switch partway through.

## Relative pronouns: who, which, that, whose

| Pronoun | Refers to | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **who** | people (as a subject) | the scientist **who** discovered it |
| **whom** | people (as an object, especially after a preposition) | the scientist **with whom** she worked; many **of whom** |
| **which** | things | the bridge, **which** opened in 1937 |
| **that** | things (and sometimes people) | the bridge **that** collapsed |
| **whose** | possession, for people or things | the town **whose** harbor froze |
| **where** | physical places only | the village **where** she was born |
| **when** | times | the year **when** the dam opened |

Two common traps:
- *Where* for something that isn't a place: ~~"a theory where energy is conserved"~~ → "a theory **in which** energy is conserved." *In which* works for both places and abstract ideas.
- A dangling preposition at the end: ~~"the stage which she performed on"~~ → "the stage **on which** she performed."

## Noun agreement

Nouns that refer to the same people or things should match in number: "The **founders** later became successful **publishers**," not "a successful publisher." And "*X, along with Y,*" is still singular: "The captain, along with her crew, **was** honored."

> The subject is "leafcutter ants," which is plural. The rest of the sentence also uses "they" and "their."

- [ ] they're
- [x] its

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The study followed 300 volunteers, many of ____ had never exercised regularly before the program began.
- [ ] them
- [ ] who
- [x] whom
- [ ] which
> After the preposition "of," the object form "whom" is required for people. "Many of them" would create a comma splice, and "which" doesn't refer to people.
:::

## Summary

- Find the antecedent by meaning, not by proximity; match singular/plural.
- *Its* is singular; collective nouns are singular.
- Prefer a clear noun over an ambiguous pronoun.
- *Who/whom* for people, *which* for things, *where* only for places, *in which* for both.
