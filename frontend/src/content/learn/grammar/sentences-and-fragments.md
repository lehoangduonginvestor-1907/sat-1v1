Almost every punctuation question on the SAT comes down to one question: **is this group of words a complete sentence or not?** Periods and semicolons need a complete sentence on both sides; commas, colons, and dashes have their own requirements. If you can recognize a sentence instantly, most Boundaries questions become mechanical.

## What makes a sentence

:::rule Definition
A sentence (an independent clause) contains a **subject** and a **conjugated verb** that goes with it, and it is not turned into a dependent clause by a word like *because*, *although*, *which*, or *who*.
:::

That's all. A sentence does not have to be long, interesting, or even make sense out of context. "Many consider it unfinished" is a perfectly good sentence. So is "It worked."

Lots of things can be added to a sentence without breaking it:

- **Prepositional phrases**, anywhere: "*After the storm,* the crews *in the northern district* restored power *within hours*."
- **Adverbs and transitions**: "The crews, *however*, restored power quickly."
- **Extra information between commas**: "The crews, *who had worked all night*, restored power."
- **Descriptive phrases**: "*Exhausted but relieved,* the crews restored power."

None of those additions can be a sentence on their own, but none of them stop the sentence from being complete.

## The disguises a fragment wears

A fragment is a group of words punctuated like a sentence that isn't one. The SAT uses the same few disguises over and over.

### 1. The *-ing* word pretending to be a verb
An *-ing* word by itself is never a conjugated verb. "The volunteers *planting* trees along the river" has a subject but no main verb.

:::examples
✗ The volunteers planting trees along the river. // No conjugated verb.
✓ The volunteers planted trees along the river. // "Planted" is conjugated.
✓ The volunteers were planting trees along the river. // "Were planting" is conjugated.
:::

Choices containing **being** ("the plan being approved") are almost always wrong on the SAT.

### 2. The *to* form
"The goal *to reduce* waste in the cafeteria" has no main verb. The infinitive *to reduce* doesn't count.

### 3. The subject with a description but no verb
This is the sneakiest one. A subject followed by a *which/who* clause or another description can look complete because there *is* a verb, but that verb belongs to the description.

:::examples
✗ The lighthouse, which was built in 1871 and still guides ships today. // "Was built" and "guides" belong to the which-clause. The lighthouse itself never does anything.
✓ The lighthouse, which was built in 1871, still guides ships today. // Now "guides" is the main verb.
✗ The lighthouse built in 1871 on the rocky northern cape. // "Built" here describes the lighthouse; there's no main verb.
:::

Cross out the description. If what's left ("The lighthouse") has no verb, it's a fragment.

### 4. The dependent clause
A clause that begins with a subordinating conjunction (*because, although, while, when, since, if, unless, whereas, after, before*) cannot stand alone, even though it has a subject and verb.

:::examples
✗ Because the river flooded every spring. // Dependent.
✓ Because the river flooded every spring, farmers built their homes on stilts. // Dependent clause + main clause.
:::

### 5. *Which*, *who*, *whose*, *where* at the start
A statement that begins with one of these words (and isn't a question) is a fragment: "Which made the results difficult to repeat." Attach it to the previous sentence with a comma instead.

### 6. "Many of which" vs. "many of them"
- "Many of **them** are endangered" is a sentence (pronoun subject + verb).
- "Many of **which** are endangered" is a dependent clause. It needs a comma and a sentence before it: "The island has dozens of bird species, many of which are endangered."

## FANBOYS at the start

A clause that begins with *and*, *but*, *or*, *so*, *yet* can't be joined to the previous sentence with a period or semicolon on the SAT ("The plan was approved. And construction began." is treated as wrong). The comma-plus-FANBOYS pattern is covered in the next lesson.

:::tip When the choices are forms of one verb
When the four choices are different forms of the same verb (*explore / exploring / to explore / explored*), ask whether the sentence already has a main verb. If it doesn't, you need a conjugated form. If it does, you probably need a descriptive *-ing* or *to* form instead, so you don't end up with two main verbs and no conjunction.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The ancient canal system, stretching more than 300 kilometers across the plain, ____ water to fields that would otherwise have been desert.
- [ ] carrying
- [ ] to carry
- [x] carried
- [ ] having carried
> The subject is "the ancient canal system," and "stretching more than 300 kilometers across the plain" is just a description. The sentence still needs a main verb, and only "carried" is conjugated.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The archive contains thousands of letters written by sailors, ____ have never been published.
- [ ] most of them
- [x] most of which
- [ ] most of these letters
- [ ] most of whom
> After the comma we need a dependent clause, because a comma alone can't join two sentences. "Most of which have never been published" is dependent. A and C would create a second complete sentence joined only by a comma (a comma splice). D is dependent too, but "whom" refers to people, and letters are things.
:::

:::check
Which of these is a complete sentence?
- [ ] The engineer who designed the first suspension bridge in the region.
- [ ] Designing the first suspension bridge in the region.
- [x] She designed the first suspension bridge in the region.
- [ ] Although she designed the first suspension bridge in the region.
> C has a subject ("she") and a conjugated verb ("designed") with nothing making it dependent. In A, "designed" belongs to the who-clause, so "the engineer" never gets a verb.
:::

## Summary

- Sentence = subject + conjugated verb, not made dependent.
- *-ing*, *to* + verb, and *being* are not conjugated verbs.
- Cross out descriptions (*which/who* clauses, phrases between commas) to see whether the subject still has a verb.
- Subordinating conjunctions and *which/who* make a clause dependent; "many of which" is dependent, "many of them" is not.
