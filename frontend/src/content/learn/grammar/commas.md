Commas show up in almost every Boundaries question, but the SAT tests a fairly short list of situations. This lesson covers the comma uses that haven't come up yet, and, just as importantly, the places where a comma is **never** allowed. On the digital SAT, a surprising number of correct answers contain **no punctuation at all**.

## When to use a comma

### Lists of three or more
Separate the items with commas: "The kit includes a compass, a map, and a whistle." The final comma before *and* (the Oxford comma) is optional, and the SAT won't make you choose based on it alone.

Never put a comma directly **after** *and* in a list unless an interrupter follows: "…a map, and, in winter, a thermal blanket." If list items contain commas themselves, semicolons can separate the items instead, but don't mix commas and semicolons as separators in the same list.

### Coordinate adjectives
Two adjectives that each describe the noun independently get a comma: "a *bold, colorful* mural." Test: if you can reverse them or put *and* between them ("colorful and bold mural"), use a comma.

Adjectives that build on each other don't: "a *traditional Japanese* garden" (you wouldn't say "Japanese and traditional garden"). And never put a comma between the last adjective and the noun: ~~"a bold, colorful, mural."~~

### After introductory words and phrases
"*However,* the plan changed." "*In the spring of 1912,* the ship set sail." "*After a long delay,* the results arrived."

But subordinating conjunctions are different: they introduce a clause and are never followed directly by a comma. ~~"Although, the plan changed…"~~ is wrong; "Although the plan changed, …" is right.

## When NOT to use a comma

These are the misuses the SAT tests most:

:::rule No comma…
1. **Between a subject and its verb**, no matter how long the subject is.
2. **Between a verb and its object** or between a verb and a *that* clause.
3. **Before or after a preposition** (unless the preposition starts an interrupter that needs commas anyway).
4. **Between the final adjective and the noun.**
5. **Between two items joined by *and*** when they aren't complete sentences.
6. **Around words ending in *-self* / *-selves*** used for emphasis.
:::

### 1. Subject and verb
Long subjects tempt you to pause, but a comma can't separate a subject from its verb.

:::examples
✗ The number of students who signed up for the robotics workshop last summer, was the highest in the school's history.
✓ The number of students who signed up for the robotics workshop last summer was the highest in the school's history.
✗ What surprised the researchers most, was the speed of the change.
✓ What surprised the researchers most was the speed of the change.
:::

The only exception is a non-essential element sitting between them, which needs a comma on **both** sides.

### 2. Verb and object
:::examples
✗ The survey found, that most residents supported the park.
✓ The survey found that most residents supported the park.
✗ The team analyzed, soil samples from twelve sites.
✓ The team analyzed soil samples from twelve sites.
:::

A colon after a verb is wrong for the same reason (~~"The team analyzed: soil samples…"~~).

### 3. Prepositions
:::examples
✗ The bridge was designed by, a team of three engineers.
✗ The bridge was designed, by a team of three engineers.
✓ The bridge was designed by a team of three engineers.
:::

### 5. Compound items
"The volunteers cleaned the beach and recorded the trash." No comma, because "recorded the trash" isn't a complete sentence. Quick test: replace ", and" with a period. If the second half can't stand alone, delete the comma.

### 6. *-self* words
"The city **itself** was founded much earlier." No commas around *itself*.

:::tip When you see four comma choices
If the choices are *word* / *word,* / *word:* / *word—*, start by asking whether anything needs to be separated at all. Check: is the blank between a subject and a verb, a verb and its object, a preposition and its object? Then the answer is the version with no punctuation.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The collection of more than four thousand handwritten recipes that a historian discovered in a farmhouse ____ provides a rare record of everyday cooking in the 1800s.
- [ ] attic,
- [ ] attic—
- [x] attic
- [ ] attic:
> The long subject is "The collection of more than four thousand handwritten recipes that a historian discovered in a farmhouse attic," and its verb is "provides." Nothing may separate a subject from its verb.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? To make the paint, the artist ground the stones into a fine powder and mixed it ____ egg yolk and water.
- [ ] with,
- [ ] with:
- [x] with
- [ ] with—
> "With" is a preposition followed by its object ("egg yolk and water"). No punctuation can come between them.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The museum's newest exhibit features a towering, ____ sculpture made entirely from recycled bicycle parts.
- [ ] intricate,
- [x] intricate
- [ ] intricate;
- [ ] intricate—
> "Towering" and "intricate" are coordinate adjectives (you could say "intricate and towering"), and the comma between them is already there. But no comma can come between the last adjective and the noun "sculpture."
:::

## Summary

- Use commas for lists, coordinate adjectives, and introductory words/phrases.
- No comma between subject and verb, verb and object, preposition and object, last adjective and noun.
- No comma before *and* joining two things that aren't sentences.
- No comma right after *although*/*because*.
- When in doubt, test whether the words on each side actually need separating; often the answer has no punctuation.
