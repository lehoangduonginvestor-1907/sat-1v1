In the last lesson both sides of the blank were complete sentences. Here, one side is a complete sentence and the other is **not**: a dependent clause, a descriptive phrase, a list, or an explanation. The rule that decides these questions is short.

:::rule Sentence + fragment
- You **can** use a comma, a colon, or a dash (depending on what the fragment does).
- You **cannot** use a period or a semicolon. Both require a complete sentence on each side.
:::

So when the choices include a period or semicolon, first check whether the second part can stand alone. If it can't, cross those choices out immediately.

## Dependent clauses

A dependent clause begins with a subordinating conjunction: *because, although, though, even though, while, whereas, when, since, if, unless, until, after, before, as*.

- **Dependent clause first → comma before the main clause.**
  "*When the tide goes out,* the pools fill with tiny crabs."
- **Main clause first → usually no comma.**
  "The pools fill with tiny crabs *when the tide goes out*."
- Exception: contrast words (*although, though, even though, whereas*, and *while* meaning "whereas") can take a comma even when they come second: "The tiles look like marble, *although they are made of clay*."

:::examples
✗ The pools fill with tiny crabs. When the tide goes out. // The second part is a dependent clause, so it can't stand alone.
✗ The pools fill with tiny crabs; when the tide goes out. // Same problem: a semicolon needs a sentence after it.
✓ When the tide goes out, the pools fill with tiny crabs.
:::

## Descriptive phrases

A sentence can be followed by a phrase that describes it or adds detail, joined with a comma:

- **-ing phrase**: "The composer worked for years without a piano, *writing every note by ear*."
- **appositive / noun phrase**: "The town relies on a single industry, *salt harvesting*."
- **with + noun + -ing**: "The glacier is retreating quickly, *with some sections losing several meters a year*." (A compact alternative to ", and some sections are losing…")

## *Which* and "all of which"

A *which* clause that describes the noun right before it follows a comma: "The team found a new mineral, *which they named after the village*."

Group words follow the same pattern:
- "…, **most of which** are fragile." ✓ (dependent: comma is correct)
- "…, **most of them** are fragile." ✗ (a full sentence after a comma = comma splice)
- "…; **most of them** are fragile." ✓ (semicolon + full sentence)

## The double-conjunction error

Two clauses need **one** joining word, not two. If the sentence already starts with *although* or *because*, the second clause can't also start with *but* or *so*.

:::examples
✗ Although the samples were small, but the results were striking. // Two conjunctions for two clauses.
✓ Although the samples were small, the results were striking.
✓ The samples were small, but the results were striking.
:::

This error is easy to miss when a long phrase separates the two parts ("Although the samples, collected over two summers in a remote valley, were small, but…"). When the blank comes in the second half, scan back to the start of the sentence for a conjunction that's already there.

## Colons

:::rule Colon
1. What comes **before** a colon must be a complete sentence.
2. What comes **after** it explains, defines, or lists something from the first part. It can be a full sentence or just a word, phrase, or list.
:::

:::examples
✓ The recipe called for three spices: cumin, coriander, and saffron. // Complete sentence before, list after.
✓ The expedition faced one serious obstacle: the weather. // A single noun after the colon is fine.
✗ The recipe called for: cumin, coriander, and saffron. // "The recipe called for" is not a complete sentence.
✗ The festival includes events such as: concerts, film screenings, and lectures. // Never put a colon after "such as" or "including."
:::

Also avoid a colon directly after a verb (~~"The problem is: the budget"~~) or a preposition (~~"made of: clay and straw"~~).

## Single dashes

A **single** dash works like a colon: it needs a complete sentence before it and introduces an explanation, example, or dramatic addition. "The city had built everything for the parade except one thing—a route." The SAT won't ask you to choose between a colon and a dash for style alone; if both appear and both would work, look for a different difference.

(Pairs of dashes, which set off extra information in the middle of a sentence, are the topic of the next lesson.)

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? Many insects can see ultraviolet ____ markings on some flower petals, invisible to human eyes, guide bees directly to the nectar.
- [ ] light, ultraviolet
- [x] light: ultraviolet
- [ ] light ultraviolet
- [ ] light, which ultraviolet
> Two complete sentences meet here ("Many insects can see ultraviolet light" / "ultraviolet markings… guide bees…"), and the second illustrates the first, so a colon works. A is a comma splice, C a run-on, and D is ungrammatical.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The coastal village depends on a single seasonal ____ the harvesting of kelp, which is dried and sold to fertilizer companies.
- [ ] industry;
- [ ] industry, that being
- [x] industry:
- [ ] industry, it is
> "The harvesting of kelp…" is a noun phrase, not a sentence, so the semicolon in A is out. B relies on "being," which the SAT treats as wordy and ungrammatical here. D is a comma splice ("it is the harvesting…" is a full sentence). The colon introduces the explanation directly.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? Although the first version of the app had only a few hundred ____ the developers kept improving it for three more years.
- [ ] users, but
- [ ] users. And
- [x] users,
- [ ] users; and
> The sentence already begins with "Although," so the second clause can't add another conjunction. A, B, and D all add "but" or "and." Dependent clause first → comma → main clause.
:::

## Summary

- Sentence + fragment: comma, colon, or dash; never a period or semicolon.
- Dependent clause first takes a comma; main clause first usually doesn't (except contrast words).
- "…, most of which" ✓ / "…, most of them" ✗.
- One conjunction per pair of clauses.
- A colon needs a complete sentence before it; never after *such as*, *including*, a verb, or a preposition.
