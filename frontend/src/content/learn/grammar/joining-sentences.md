This is the most frequently tested punctuation skill on the SAT. A Boundaries question will often give you four choices that differ only in the punctuation between two words, like *harvest. The* / *harvest, the* / *harvest; the* / *harvest the*. Your job is to recognize whether two complete sentences meet at the blank and, if they do, to pick a legal way to connect them.

## The four legal joins

When the words on both sides of the blank are complete sentences (independent clauses), you have exactly these options:

:::rule Joining two independent clauses
1. **Period**: Sentence one. Sentence two.
2. **Semicolon**: Sentence one; sentence two. (A semicolon works exactly like a period.)
3. **Comma + FANBOYS** (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*): Sentence one, and sentence two.
4. **Colon or single dash**, but only when the second sentence explains, illustrates, or spells out the first: Sentence one: sentence two.
:::

And these are always wrong:

- **Comma alone** (a *comma splice*): ~~The river rose overnight, the bridge was closed by morning.~~
- **Nothing at all** (a *run-on*): ~~The river rose overnight the bridge was closed by morning.~~
- **FANBOYS with no comma**: when *and*, *but*, *so* (etc.) join two complete sentences, the SAT expects a comma before the conjunction: ~~The river rose overnight and the bridge was closed by morning.~~ → *overnight, and the bridge*.

## Step one: find the whole sentence

The blank is often in the middle of a long sentence, and the clause boundary is not always where you'd expect. Always read from the **capital letter** at the start of the sentence to the **period** at the end, then ask: what is the subject and verb on each side of the blank?

### Watch the openers of sentence two
Some phrases sound like the end of the first sentence but grammatically belong to the beginning of the second. The SAT loves to put the blank right before one of them.

:::examples
✓ The two teams spent a decade mapping the reef. **By the time the survey ended,** they had identified 300 new species. // The time phrase begins the second sentence.
✓ The festival drew record crowds. **Featuring musicians from 40 countries,** it filled every hotel in the city. // The -ing phrase describes "it," the subject of sentence two.
✓ The museum was closed for repairs. **Because the roof leaked,** several paintings had been moved to storage. // A dependent clause opening sentence two.
:::

In each case the period goes **before** the phrase, not after it.

## Transition words do not join sentences

Words like *however*, *therefore*, *thus*, *consequently*, *moreover*, *nevertheless*, *instead*, *meanwhile*, and time words like *today*, *later*, *then*, *currently*, *now*, *first*, *finally* are adverbs. They connect ideas logically but cannot connect two sentences grammatically. When one of them starts the second clause, the punctuation before it must be a period or semicolon.

:::examples
✗ The trail is steep, however, most hikers reach the summit by noon. // Comma splice: "however" doesn't fix it.
✓ The trail is steep; however, most hikers reach the summit by noon.
✓ The trail is steep. However, most hikers reach the summit by noon.
✗ The company tested the drone in the desert, today it delivers medicine to remote clinics. // "Today it delivers…" is a new sentence.
✓ The company tested the drone in the desert; today it delivers medicine to remote clinics.
:::

## Comma splice red flags

When you see a comma followed by any of these, check carefully for a splice:

- a **pronoun subject**: *, it* / *, they* / *, she* / *, this* / *, these* / *, one*
- a **name or noun** followed directly by a verb: *, the committee voted*
- a **conjunctive adverb** or time word: *, however* / *, therefore* / *, today*

## More rules the SAT tests

### No FANBOYS after a period or semicolon
On the SAT, a period or semicolon followed by *and* or *but* is treated as wrong ("The tests were finished; and the results were clear"). Use one or the other: a semicolon **or** comma + FANBOYS.

### One subject, two verbs: no comma
If the second half doesn't have its own subject, it isn't a sentence, so it doesn't get a comma before *and*.

:::examples
✓ The volunteers cleaned the beach and recorded every piece of plastic they found. // One subject ("volunteers") with two verbs.
✗ The volunteers cleaned the beach, and recorded every piece of plastic they found. // No second subject, so no comma.
:::

To test it, replace ", and" with a period. If the second part can't stand alone, remove the comma.

### The "equivalent choices" shortcut
A period, a semicolon, and comma + FANBOYS are interchangeable whenever two sentences meet. A question has only one correct answer, so **if two of these appear among the choices and both would work, both must be wrong**. The real answer is something else, usually because the second half isn't actually a sentence.

### Colon vs. semicolon
Both need a complete sentence before them. A colon promises that what follows explains or illustrates what came before ("The plan had one flaw: no one had checked the budget"). If the second sentence just adds a related point, use a semicolon or period instead.

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? Glass frogs have nearly transparent skin on their undersides ____ observers can see the frogs' hearts beating through their bellies.
- [ ] undersides,
- [ ] undersides
- [x] undersides;
- [ ] undersides, which
> Both halves are complete sentences ("Glass frogs have…" / "observers can see…"). A creates a comma splice and B a run-on. D is grammatical only if "which" can refer back to something, but "which observers can see the frogs' hearts" doesn't make sense. A semicolon joins the two sentences correctly.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The first printing of the atlas sold out within a week ____ the publisher ordered a second printing of twice the size.
- [ ] week, therefore,
- [ ] week, therefore
- [x] week; therefore,
- [ ] week therefore
> "Therefore" is a transition, not a conjunction, and the publisher clause is a complete sentence. The only legal pattern is semicolon + therefore + comma.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? The volunteers spent the morning counting shorebirds along the ____ the afternoon, they moved inland to survey the marsh.
- [ ] coast, in
- [ ] coast in
- [x] coast. In
- [ ] coast, in which
> "In the afternoon" sounds like it could end the first sentence, but it opens the second one: "In the afternoon, they moved inland…" is a complete sentence, and so is "The volunteers spent the morning counting shorebirds along the coast." A is a comma splice, B a run-on, and D makes no sense ("in which the afternoon").
:::

## Summary

- Legal joins for two sentences: period, semicolon, comma + FANBOYS, and colon/dash when the second explains the first.
- Never a comma alone, never nothing.
- Transition words (*however, therefore, today*) don't join sentences; they need a period or semicolon before them.
- Read the full sentence: the second clause may start with a phrase or dependent clause.
- If two equivalent joins both seem to work, neither is the answer.
