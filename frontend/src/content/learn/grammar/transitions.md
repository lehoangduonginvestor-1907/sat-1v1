Transitions are the single most common Writing question on the digital SAT: expect several in every Reading and Writing module, near the end. Each one gives you a short passage with a blank between two ideas and four connecting words or phrases. Grammar never decides the answer here, because all four choices fit the sentence. What decides it is **logic**: which word correctly describes how the second idea relates to the first?

## The core move: decide before you look

The biggest mistake students make is reading the four choices first and "trying each one out." Transition words are slippery, and after a few tries everything starts to sound acceptable. Instead:

:::steps
1. Read the passage from the beginning, not just the sentence with the blank.
2. Cover the choices. In your own words, say how the sentence after the blank relates to the sentence before it: **same direction**, **opposite direction**, or **cause and effect**?
3. Only now look at the choices. Eliminate every word from the wrong family.
4. If two words survive, look for the more precise relationship (for example, an *example* versus a simple *addition*).
:::

## The three families

Almost every transition belongs to one of three families. Learn the family, not just the word.

| Family | What it signals | Common words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Continue** (same direction) | adds a point, gives an example, restates, emphasizes, compares | furthermore, moreover, in addition, also, additionally; for example, for instance, specifically; that is, in other words; indeed, in fact; similarly, likewise |
| **Cause and effect** | the second idea is a result of the first | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence, accordingly, as such |
| **Contrast** (opposite direction) | the second idea goes against, limits, or surprises | however, nevertheless, nonetheless, still, even so, yet, in contrast, conversely, on the other hand, instead, rather, by contrast, meanwhile, regardless |

There is also a small **sequence** group (first, then, next, finally, subsequently, previously, later) that orders events in time.

:::examples
✓ The new bridge cut commuting times in half. **As a result**, home prices on the far side of the river rose sharply. // Faster commutes caused the price increase: cause and effect.
✗ The new bridge cut commuting times in half. **However**, home prices on the far side of the river rose sharply. // Nothing about rising prices contradicts faster commutes, so a contrast word is illogical.
✓ Most moths are active at night. Some species, **however**, feed only during the day. // "Most do X" versus "some do the opposite" is a contrast.
:::

## Fine distinctions inside each family

Once you have the family, the test sometimes asks you to choose between two words that are close cousins. These are the distinctions that matter most.

### Example vs. addition
*For example* and *for instance* introduce a specific case of a general claim that was just made. *Furthermore* and *moreover* add a **new** point of equal weight. If the second sentence is a case of the first, choose the example word.

:::examples
✓ Many desert plants store water in unusual ways. **For instance**, the barrel cactus swells after a rainstorm. // The cactus is one case of "plants that store water."
✗ Many desert plants store water in unusual ways. **Furthermore**, the barrel cactus swells after a rainstorm. // The second sentence is not a separate, additional claim.
:::

### Emphasis and restatement
*In fact* and *indeed* push the previous idea further ("not only that, but even more so"). *That is* and *in other words* restate or define the previous idea in clearer terms; they add nothing new.

### Similarity
*Similarly* and *likewise* require two different things doing the same thing: "Bees communicate through movement. *Likewise*, some fish signal by changing color."

### Plain contrast vs. concession
*However*, *in contrast*, and *by contrast* simply mark a difference. *Nevertheless*, *nonetheless*, *still*, and *even so* mean "despite what was just said," so they need a first idea that would normally lead you to expect the opposite.

:::examples
✓ The experiment was expensive and slow. **Nevertheless**, the team chose to repeat it three times. // Cost and slowness would make you expect them not to repeat it.
✓ The northern colony relied on fishing. **In contrast**, the southern colony relied on farming. // Two different situations side by side: no "despite" relationship.
:::

### Other words worth knowing
- *Meanwhile* means "at the same time" and often introduces a contrasting situation happening simultaneously.
- *Instead* and *rather* mean that one thing replaces another ("She didn't fly. *Instead*, she took the train.").
- *As such* means "in that role" and must refer back to a noun that was just named ("He was the team's captain. *As such*, he spoke for the players.").
- *Subsequently* means "afterward," a pure time word; *previously* means "before."
- *Regardless* means "without being affected by what was just said."

## Where the transition sits

A transition does not have to start the sentence. It can sit after the subject ("The data, **however**, told a different story") or at the very end ("The data told a different story, **however**."). Wherever it appears between commas, it still connects **this sentence to the previous one**. Read the previous sentence to decide.

:::trap Read the whole passage
Many transition questions are written so that the sentence with the blank looks like a contrast or a result on its own, but the *previous* sentence changes the picture. Always start reading at the first sentence. The passages are short; reading all of them costs only seconds.
:::

## Elimination shortcuts

- If the two ideas point in the **same** direction, cross out every contrast word at once.
- If they point in **opposite** directions, cross out every continue word and every result word.
- If two choices mean exactly the same thing (for example *therefore* and *thus*) and nothing else separates them, neither can be the single correct answer. This is a tie-breaker, not a first step.

:::check
Rice farmers in the region traditionally flooded their fields for most of the growing season. ____ a new technique that lets fields dry out between waterings has cut water use by nearly a third without lowering harvests.
- [ ] For example,
- [ ] Similarly,
- [x] Recently, however,
- [ ] As a result,
> The first sentence describes the traditional practice; the second describes a newer approach that departs from it. That is a contrast, and "recently" also marks the shift from old to new. The new technique is not an example of flooding, not similar to it, and not caused by it.
:::

:::check
The violinist practiced the difficult passage for months before the premiere. ____ she still asked the conductor to slow the tempo during the final rehearsal.
- [ ] Therefore,
- [x] Even so,
- [ ] For instance,
- [ ] In other words,
> Months of practice would lead you to expect she didn't need a slower tempo. "Even so" means "despite that," which is exactly the relationship. "Therefore" would claim the practice *caused* the request, which is illogical.
:::

:::check
Some spiders build a new web every evening and eat the old silk to recycle its protein. ____ almost none of the material is wasted from one night to the next.
- [ ] Nevertheless,
- [ ] Meanwhile,
- [x] Consequently,
- [ ] In contrast,
> Eating the old silk is the cause; nothing being wasted is the result. "Consequently" is a cause-and-effect word.
:::

:::check
The museum's new science wing is designed to be entirely hands-on: visitors are invited to touch, build, and take apart every object in it. ____ nothing in the wing is kept behind glass.
- [ ] However,
- [ ] For example,
- [x] In other words,
- [ ] Nevertheless,
> "Nothing is behind glass" says the same thing as "every object can be touched," just from another angle. A restatement calls for "in other words." It is not a specific case of the first idea (so not "for example"), and nothing contrasts.
:::

## Summary

- Decide the relationship (continue, cause/effect, contrast) **before** reading the choices.
- Within a family, separate example from addition and plain contrast from "despite this."
- A transition in the middle or at the end of a sentence still links that sentence to the one before it.
- Read the passage from its first sentence every time.
