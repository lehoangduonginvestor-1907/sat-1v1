Writers often drop extra information into the middle of a sentence: a description, a name, a side comment. The SAT tests whether that extra information is fenced off correctly. This is one of the most heavily tested Boundaries topics, and the rules are completely consistent once you learn them.

## What "non-essential" means

:::rule The removal test
A **non-essential** element is a word, phrase, or clause that can be lifted out of the sentence without breaking the grammar or the basic meaning. It must be set off **on both sides** with matching punctuation: two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses.
:::

:::examples
✓ The Great Library, **which was founded in the third century BCE,** held hundreds of thousands of scrolls. // Remove the clause: "The Great Library held hundreds of thousands of scrolls." Still a sentence.
✓ Octavia Butler**—a writer who mixed science fiction with history—**won a MacArthur Fellowship in 1995. // Two dashes.
✓ The fossil (**discovered by a ten-year-old**) turned out to be a new species. // Two parentheses.
:::

Common types of non-essential elements:
- *which* and *who* clauses: "…, which was restored in 2010, …"
- **appositives**, nouns that rename the noun before them: "Lagos, *Nigeria's largest city*, …"
- descriptive phrases: "The bridge, *completed in 1932*, …"
- interrupters and transitions: "…, *however*, …" / "…, *critics agree*, …" / "…, *for example*, …"

## Match the pair

The opening and closing marks must be the same type. Never open with a dash and close with a comma, or open with a comma and close with a parenthesis.

:::examples
✗ The bridge—completed in 1932, carried more traffic than any other in the state.
✓ The bridge—completed in 1932—carried more traffic than any other in the state.
✓ The bridge, completed in 1932, carried more traffic than any other in the state.
:::

## The blank may be at either end

SAT questions often show you only one end of the element and blank out the other. So:

- If the blank is at the **start** of an element, read ahead to find how it closes.
- If the blank is at the **end**, look back for how it opened. If you see an opening dash earlier in the sentence, the answer almost certainly contains a closing dash.

The element usually ends right before the **main verb** of the sentence. "The archive, *which holds letters from three centuries* **contains**…" → the closing comma goes right before *contains*.

:::trap Cross out, then check
After you cross out the non-essential element, the remaining sentence must be grammatical. Errors that hide behind the extra information:

- ~~The archive, which holds letters from three centuries, **and contains** maps.~~ (no main verb once the conjunction is removed)
- ~~The archive, which holds letters from three centuries, **it contains** maps.~~ (two subjects)
- ~~The archive, which holds letters from three centuries, **containing** maps.~~ (*-ing* isn't a main verb)
:::

## Two commas don't always mean "non-essential"

Sometimes two commas in a sentence have nothing to do with each other: one ends an introductory phrase, another sits before a FANBOYS. Use the removal test. If removing the words between the commas breaks the sentence, those commas weren't a pair.

## Transitions in the middle of a sentence

A transition can sit inside a sentence between two commas: "Nearly a thousand years later, **however**, the castle still stands." That works because everything around *however* is one sentence.

It fails when *however* sits between **two** sentences:

:::examples
✗ The castle survived the war, however, its library was destroyed. // Two complete sentences around "however": a comma splice.
✓ The castle survived the war; however, its library was destroyed.
✓ The castle survived the war. Its library, however, was destroyed.
:::

When a transition is blanked between two clauses, decide which sentence it belongs to. Read both clauses without it; if they're two complete sentences, the transition goes **after** a period or semicolon (starting sentence two) or **before** one (ending sentence one). Pick the version whose logic fits: does the contrast belong with the previous sentence or the following one?

## Essential information: no commas

Some information is necessary to identify which thing you mean. It gets **no** commas.

### *That* clauses are always essential
"The house **that** my grandfather built still stands." No comma before or after a *that* clause (rare exception: an interrupter inside it).

### Essential vs. non-essential *who*
- "Students **who** finish early may leave." (No commas: only the students who finish early, not all students.)
- "The students, **who** had finished early, left the room." (Commas: all the students had finished; the clause just adds information.)

### Names and titles
A name right after a description is the hardest version of this rule. The test: commas around the name mean "the only one"; no commas mean "one of several."

:::examples
✓ The novel's author, **Jamaica Kincaid,** was born in Antigua. // A novel has one author, so the name is extra: two commas.
✓ The author **Jamaica Kincaid** was born in Antigua. // "The author" alone doesn't tell us which author; the name is essential: no commas.
✓ Marine biologist **Ana Ruiz** led the expedition. // A job title used like a label before a name never takes commas.
✗ Marine biologist, Ana Ruiz, led the expedition. // Remove the name: "Marine biologist led the expedition" isn't a sentence.
✗ The novel's author, Jamaica Kincaid was born in Antigua. // One comma: the pair is broken.
:::

Rule of thumb: **two commas or none**, never one. Then cross out the name. If the sentence still makes sense, use two commas; if it falls apart, use none.

## Summary

- Non-essential = removable; set off on both sides with matching marks.
- Find the other end of the element; it usually closes before the main verb.
- After crossing out, the sentence must still be grammatical.
- A transition can sit between commas only inside one sentence.
- *That* clauses, defining *who* clauses, and labels before names are essential: no commas.
- Names: two commas or none.
