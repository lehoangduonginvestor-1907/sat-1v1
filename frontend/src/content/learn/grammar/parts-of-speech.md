You don't need to diagram sentences to do well on SAT grammar, but you do need a handful of labels. The punctuation and grammar lessons that follow constantly ask questions like "is this word a verb?" or "does this group of words have a subject?" This short refresher gives you exactly the vocabulary those lessons use.

## Verbs

A **verb** expresses an action (*build*, *migrate*, *argue*) or a state of being (*is*, *seem*, *remain*). The SAT cares about one distinction above all others:

- A **conjugated** (finite) verb changes form to match its subject and shows tense: *she writes*, *they wrote*, *it has written*, *we will write*.
- A **non-conjugated** form does not: the infinitive *to write*, the *-ing* form *writing*, and the participle *written* used on its own.

Only a conjugated verb can be the main verb of a sentence. "The researchers *analyzing* the data" is not a sentence; "The researchers *analyzed* the data" is.

:::rule Two quick tests for a verb
- Can you put *to* in front of it and change its tense (*to migrate → migrated*)? Then it can be a verb.
- In the present tense, a verb with a singular third-person subject ends in **-s**: *the river flows*, *the rivers flow*. This is the opposite of nouns, where **-s** makes a plural.
:::

### The tenses you'll meet

| Tense | Example | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| simple present | the comet **returns** | general truths, habits, the present |
| simple past | the comet **returned** | a finished action at a specific time |
| present perfect | the comet **has returned** | started in the past and continues, or recent with no date given |
| past perfect | the comet **had returned** | the earlier of two past actions |
| future | the comet **will return** | the future |
| conditional | the comet **would return** | hypothetical, or a future seen from the past |

## Nouns and pronouns

A **noun** names a person, place, thing, or idea: *engineer*, *Lisbon*, *telescope*, *curiosity*. If you can put *a*, *an*, or *the* in front of a word, it is probably a noun. Abstract nouns often end in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ness*, *-ity*.

A **pronoun** stands in for a noun: *it*, *they*, *she*, *this*, *which*, *who*, *each*, *one*. Pronouns can be subjects ("*They* left early"), which matters a lot when you're deciding whether a group of words is a complete sentence.

## Adjectives and adverbs

- An **adjective** describes a noun: a *fragile* shell, the shell is *fragile*.
- An **adverb** describes a verb, an adjective, or a whole clause: *quickly*, *very*, *often*.
- **Conjunctive adverbs** are the big transition words: *however*, *therefore*, *moreover*, *consequently*, *nevertheless*, *thus*. They connect ideas but **cannot join two sentences with just a comma**. You'll see why in the lesson on joining sentences.

## Prepositions

A **preposition** shows position, time, or relationship and is followed by a noun: *in* the lab, *after* the storm, *between* the two tests, *of* the team, *during* the war, *according to* the report. A prepositional phrase never contains the subject of the sentence, so you can cross it out when hunting for the subject.

## Conjunctions

**Coordinating conjunctions** join equal parts. There are only seven, remembered as **FANBOYS**: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*.

**Subordinating conjunctions** make a clause dependent, so it can no longer stand alone as a sentence: *because, although, though, even though, while, whereas, when, since, if, unless, until, after, before, as*.

:::examples
✓ The test was difficult, **but** most students finished. // FANBOYS joining two complete sentences.
✓ **Although** the test was difficult, most students finished. // "Although the test was difficult" can't stand alone; it leans on the main clause.
✗ Although the test was difficult. // A dependent clause by itself is a fragment.
:::

## Clauses and phrases

- A **clause** has a subject and a conjugated verb. An **independent clause** can stand alone as a sentence ("the bees returned"). A **dependent clause** cannot, usually because it starts with a subordinating conjunction or a word like *which* or *who* ("because the bees returned," "which the bees ignored").
- A **phrase** is a group of words without a subject–verb pair: "in the early morning," "having finished the survey," "a species of tiny frog."

:::check
In the sentence "The tiny seeds, carried by the wind, spread across the valley," which word is the main (conjugated) verb?
- [ ] carried
- [x] spread
- [ ] seeds
- [ ] across
> "Carried by the wind" is a participial phrase describing the seeds. "Spread" is the conjugated verb that tells what the seeds did. "Seeds" is the subject, and "across" is a preposition.
:::

:::check
Which of the following is a complete sentence?
- [ ] Because the bridge was closed for repairs.
- [ ] The bridge, which was closed for repairs.
- [x] The bridge was closed for repairs.
- [ ] The bridge being closed for repairs.
> Only C has a subject ("the bridge") with a conjugated verb ("was closed") and no word making it dependent. A starts with a subordinating conjunction, B has no main verb (the verb is inside the "which" clause), and D uses "being," which is not conjugated.
:::

:::check
Which word is a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS)?
- [ ] however
- [ ] although
- [x] yet
- [ ] therefore
> "Yet" is one of the seven FANBOYS. "However" and "therefore" are conjunctive adverbs, and "although" is a subordinating conjunction.
:::
