Main idea questions ask "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?" Related questions ask about the overall structure of a passage or which quotation illustrates its central claim. All of them require the same leap: from a mass of details to the single point those details serve.

## Start with the topic

The **topic** is the person, thing, or idea the passage is about. It's usually introduced in the first sentence or two and then mentioned again and again, often under different names (*the painter → she → her work → the canvases*).

:::rule Why the topic matters
The correct answer to a main idea question will be about the topic. Often it's the only choice that is.
:::

Get the scope exactly right. If the passage is about one artist, the main idea is about that artist, not "artists" in general, not her country, not the art form.

:::examples
✓ Passage about one architect whose buildings stay cool in desert heat without air conditioning → "The architect's designs keep buildings cool through their structure alone." // Right topic, right scope.
✗ Same passage → "Architects in hot climates have long experimented with new materials." // Too broad: architects in general.
✗ Same passage → "One of the architect's buildings won a national award." // A detail, not the main idea.
:::

## Then find the point: topic + "so what?"

A main point is not a topic ("bees") or a theme ("change"). It's a **claim**: what the author wants you to understand about the topic.

:::rule Main point
**Topic + so what? = main point.**
*Street trees* (topic) + *cool neighborhoods enough to reduce heat-related illness* (so what) = main point.
:::

Authors usually state the point near the beginning, then repeat or reinforce it at the end. When the point isn't stated so clearly, look for:
- words like *key*, *important*, *significant*, *central*, *the point is*, *the goal*;
- a **colon or dash**, which often introduces the crucial explanation;
- a **transition** like *however* or *but*, after which the author's real view usually appears.

### Summarize the argument, not the story
Students often "summarize" by retelling everything in order: first this, then that. That wastes time and blurs the main idea with the details. Instead, write a few words that capture only the claim, e.g., "new study: naps → better memory, not just rest." Shorthand is fine; nobody else has to read it.

## The old idea / new idea pattern

Many science and social-science passages follow the same template: **"People used to believe X, but now there's evidence for Y."** The new idea is almost always the main point.

| Signals of the old idea | Signals of the new idea |
| --- | --- |
| *Traditionally…, For decades…, It was long assumed…, Many people believe…, Some scientists argue…* | *However…, But in fact…, Recently…, New research suggests…, Actually…, But is this really the case?* |

As soon as you read "for decades, researchers assumed…," you can predict that the passage will challenge that assumption. Even a single word like *historically* hints that the situation has changed.

Track who believes what. If a passage presents an old view and a new one, an answer that attributes the old view to the author is reversed.

## Literature passages

Fiction and poetry don't make arguments, but they do have a main idea: usually a character's trait, feeling, or situation. Key information again tends to appear at the **beginning and end**. Stay literal: describe what the text actually says about the character, and don't hunt for hidden symbolism.

For **poems**, find the plain statement behind the images. A poem that describes a frozen, silent field where seeds wait under the snow has a main idea like "what looks lifeless is quietly preparing for growth."

## Structure questions

"Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?" Summarize what each part of the passage does (introduces a claim, gives an example, describes a reaction…). Then check one simple part of each choice, such as what happens at the end, and eliminate any choice that gets it wrong.

:::check
"For much of the twentieth century, historians described the medieval period as an age of scientific stagnation in Europe. Recent scholarship paints a different picture. Researchers have documented sophisticated advances in optics, timekeeping, and agriculture, and they have traced how scholars of the period built on Arabic and Greek texts to develop new ideas." Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- [ ] Medieval scholars relied entirely on Arabic and Greek texts.
- [x] Recent research challenges the view that the medieval period lacked scientific progress in Europe.
- [ ] Twentieth-century historians studied medieval agriculture in detail.
- [ ] The medieval period produced more scientific advances than any later period.
> Classic old idea/new idea: "for much of the twentieth century" (old) vs. "recent scholarship paints a different picture" (new). A is extreme ("entirely"), C is off-target, and D makes a comparison the passage never makes.
:::

:::check
"Ana had planned every detail of the garden party: the lanterns, the music, the long table under the oak tree. When the first drops of rain fell an hour before the guests arrived, she stood at the window for a long moment. Then she laughed, pulled the table into the barn, and hung the lanterns from the rafters." Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- [ ] Ana is disappointed when bad weather ruins her plans.
- [x] Ana adapts cheerfully when an unexpected problem threatens her plans.
- [ ] Ana regrets not having held the party indoors from the start.
- [ ] Ana's guests help her move the party into the barn.
> The key information is at the end: she laughs and moves everything into the barn. A ignores her reaction, C invents regret, and D adds guests who haven't arrived yet.
:::
