Command of Evidence (textual) questions ask you to evaluate a claim: "Which finding, if true, would most directly **support** the researchers' hypothesis?" "Which finding would most directly **weaken** the claim?" "Which quotation from the poem most effectively **illustrates** the claim?" They're inference questions with one extra step: instead of drawing a conclusion, you judge what evidence would affect a conclusion.

These questions reward patience. If you skip steps, the answer choices, which are designed to sound relevant, will pull you in.

## The three-step method

:::steps
1. **Identify the claim** and simplify it. It's usually stated in the question or in the last sentence of the passage. Rewrite it as a simple relationship: "more X → more Y," "X causes Y," "A is different from B."
2. **Decide what evidence would support or weaken it** before reading the choices. Support = shows the same relationship. Weaken = shows the relationship failing.
3. **Choose the option closest to your prediction.**
:::

### Supporting a claim
The correct finding must match the claim's specific **variables** and **direction**.

:::examples
✓ Claim: students who take handwritten notes remember lectures better than those who type. → Support: "In a follow-up test a week later, students who had handwritten their notes recalled more key concepts than those who had typed." // Same variables (handwriting vs. typing, memory), same direction.
✗ → "Students who typed their notes wrote down more words per minute." // About speed, not memory.
✗ → "Students who handwrote notes reported enjoying lectures more." // About enjoyment, not memory.
:::

### Weakening a claim
To weaken "X causes Y," show **X without Y** (the cause present, but no effect) or **Y without X** (the effect occurring without the supposed cause).

:::examples
✓ Claim: a certain soil bacterium makes tomato plants resistant to drought. → Weaken: "Tomato plants grown with the bacterium wilted during drought at the same rate as plants grown without it." // Cause present, effect missing.
✗ → "The bacterium is also found in the soil of pepper farms." // Irrelevant to whether it causes drought resistance.
:::

:::trap Right topic, wrong condition
The most tempting wrong answers use the right vocabulary but change one condition. If the claim is about *deep sleep early in the night*, a finding about *dreaming sleep late in the night* doesn't support it, even though it mentions sleep and memory. Check every specific condition in the claim: which group, which time, which variable.
:::

Also eliminate findings that show **no difference** when the claim predicts a difference, and findings that point in the **opposite direction**.

## Illustrating a claim with a quotation

These questions often go with poems, plays, and novels: "The student claims that the speaker urges the listener to keep going despite hardship. Which quotation most effectively illustrates the claim?"

- Break the claim into parts (here: *urges the listener* + *keep going* + *despite hardship*).
- The right quotation shows **all** parts. A quotation that mentions hardship but doesn't urge anyone to do anything is wrong; so is one where the speaker talks about "we" when the claim is about addressing someone else.
- If the claim involves a contrast ("appearance vs. reality"), look for contrast words (*but, yet, though*) inside the quotation.
- Old-fashioned language can be dense. Translate each quotation into plain English before deciding.

:::check
"Researchers propose that city birds sing at a higher pitch than rural birds of the same species because low-pitched songs are drowned out by traffic noise." Which finding, if true, would most directly **support** the researchers' proposal?
- [ ] City birds of the species are larger on average than rural birds.
- [x] Within the city, birds living near busy roads sing at a higher pitch than birds living in quiet parks.
- [ ] Rural birds of the species sing more often in the morning than in the evening.
- [ ] Traffic noise in the city has increased steadily over the past decade.
> The claim links traffic noise to higher pitch. B shows exactly that relationship even inside the city: more noise → higher pitch. D mentions traffic noise but says nothing about birdsong.
:::

:::check
"A nutrition researcher claims that eating breakfast improves students' concentration during morning classes." Which finding, if true, would most directly **weaken** the claim?
- [ ] Students who ate breakfast reported feeling more awake.
- [ ] Many students skip breakfast because they wake up late.
- [x] Students who ate breakfast and students who skipped it scored the same on a concentration test given in the morning.
- [ ] Students concentrate better in morning classes than in afternoon classes.
> If breakfast improves concentration, the two groups should differ. C shows the cause (breakfast) without the effect (better concentration). A supports the claim somewhat; B and D are irrelevant.
:::

:::check
A student claims that in a poem, the speaker contrasts how calm the sea appears with the danger beneath its surface. Which quotation most effectively illustrates the claim?
- [ ] "The gulls cry out above the morning tide, / And fishing boats go drifting side by side."
- [ ] "I walked the shore when I was young and free, / And dreamed of distant lands across the sea."
- [x] "So smooth it lies, as still as polished glass, / Yet rocks wait sharp below where vessels pass."
- [ ] "The storm arrived at dusk with howling breath, / And every wave was black and cold as death."
> The claim has two parts: calm appearance and hidden danger. C has both, joined by "yet": "smooth… still as polished glass" vs. "rocks wait sharp below." D shows danger but no calm; A and B show neither contrast.
:::

## Summary

- Pin down the claim; simplify it into a relationship.
- Predict what evidence would support or weaken it before reading the choices.
- Support: same variables, same direction. Weaken: cause without effect, or effect without cause.
- Illustrate: the quotation must show every part of the claim.
