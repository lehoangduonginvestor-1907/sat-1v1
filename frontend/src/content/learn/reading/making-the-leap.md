SAT passages move constantly between specific details and general labels for those details. A sentence describes an experiment; the next sentence calls it "this finding." A paragraph lists two ideas; a later sentence mentions "the latter." If you can't connect each label back to what it stands for, you lose the thread of the argument, and many questions depend on exactly that connection.

This lesson isn't about one question type. It's a reading skill that makes every question type easier.

## Pronouns

A pronoun (*it, they, this, these, its, their*) replaces a noun that appeared earlier. Writers use them to avoid repeating the same phrase over and over.

:::examples
✓ The **museum** reopened in May. Since then, **it** has welcomed more visitors than in any previous year. // "It" = the museum.
✓ **Glaciers** in the region are shrinking. **Their** meltwater feeds three major rivers. // "Their" = the glaciers'.
:::

Keep in mind:
- **Number matters.** *They* needs a plural noun; *it* a singular one. If a sentence says "they," look back for a noun ending in *-s* (or another plural).
- **The same pronoun can switch referents.** In "The **probe** photographed the **comet** as **it** passed. Days later, **it** sent the images home," the first *it* is the comet (it passed), the second is the probe (it sent images).
- **Occasionally the pronoun comes first**: "In **its** first year, the **program** trained 200 teachers."

## Summary nouns ("compression" nouns)

More challenging than pronouns are phrases like *this finding*, *such developments*, *the phenomenon*, *this approach*, *the shift*, *these concerns*. They **compress** an entire idea from earlier in the passage into a single noun.

:::examples
✓ For decades, scientists assumed that the deep ocean floor was nearly lifeless. Recent surveys have found dense communities of worms and microbes around hydrothermal vents. **This discovery** has forced a rethinking of where life can exist. // "This discovery" = that dense communities live around deep-sea vents.
:::

Without the compression noun, the writer would have to repeat the whole previous sentence. Your job when you meet one is to expand it back out.

:::rule How to decode a pronoun or summary noun
Back up. Read the sentence before it, and if necessary the one before that, until you find the idea it stands for. Never start reading at the pronoun itself and keep going forward hoping it will become clear.
:::

This matters because questions often ask about these phrases directly ("Based on the text, what is the 'concern' mentioned in the third sentence?"), or because the correct answer to a main-idea or inference question is just the expanded version of a summary noun.

## The former and the latter

When two things are mentioned, **the former** means the first one named and **the latter** means the second (*latter* sounds like *later*).

:::examples
✓ The city considered two plans: building a new bridge and expanding the ferry service. **The former** would take a decade; **the latter** could begin next year. // Former = the bridge; latter = the ferries.
:::

Writers sometimes mention the latter before the former. Don't assume order; check which item each word points to.

:::check
"Some bird species learn their songs from adults, while others are born knowing them. Researchers have found that **the former** often develop regional 'dialects.'" What does "the former" refer to?
- [ ] adult birds
- [x] species that learn their songs from adults
- [ ] species that are born knowing their songs
- [ ] researchers who study birdsong
> Two groups are named: species that learn songs and species born knowing them. "The former" is the first group named.
:::

:::check
"Coastal towns have long relied on sea walls to hold back storm surges. But engineers now warn that the walls can worsen erosion on nearby beaches by redirecting wave energy. **This drawback** has led some towns to restore wetlands instead." What is "this drawback"?
- [ ] Storm surges have grown stronger.
- [ ] Wetlands are expensive to restore.
- [x] Sea walls can increase erosion on nearby beaches.
- [ ] Engineers disagree about how to build sea walls.
> Back up one sentence: the drawback is that sea walls can worsen erosion by redirecting wave energy.
:::

:::check
"The Andean condor can soar for hours without flapping its wings. Using rising currents of warm air, **it** can travel more than 150 kilometers in a single flight." What does "it" refer to?
- [ ] a rising current of warm air
- [x] the Andean condor
- [ ] a single flight
- [ ] the condor's wings
> The one that travels using warm air currents is the condor. The phrase "using rising currents of warm air" describes the condor's method.
:::
