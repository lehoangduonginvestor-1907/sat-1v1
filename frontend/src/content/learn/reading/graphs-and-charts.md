Quantitative Command of Evidence questions pair a short passage with a table, bar graph, or line graph. They usually ask you to choose the answer that "uses data from the table/graph" to support a claim or complete a sentence. They look complex, with long technical terms, multiple data series, and long answer choices, but they're rarely as hard as they look.

## Two facts that make these easier

1. **You need no outside knowledge.** No matter how technical the topic, everything you need is on the screen. You don't have to understand every term to answer correctly.
2. **The graphic is often not the hard part.** Several answer choices will describe the graph **accurately**. Only one will match the specific claim in the passage. So the passage, not the graph, usually decides the answer.

:::rule Accurate ≠ correct
An answer can describe the data perfectly and still be wrong, because it doesn't support the particular claim the question asks about.
:::

## The method

:::steps
1. **Read the passage, especially the last sentence.** That's where the claim or the blank is. Earlier sentences are mostly context.
2. **List the conditions** the correct answer must meet. "Some populations had recovered *and* reached their highest level since 1960" = two conditions. "One drug was much less effective against one strain" = one drug, a large gap.
3. **Eliminate choices that fail a condition**, often without looking at the graph: wrong number of items, a small gap when a large one is needed, the wrong direction, the wrong group.
4. **Confirm with the graphic.** Read the title, axis labels, units, and legend, then find only the bars or lines you need.
:::

:::trap Don't start with the graph
If you study the graph first, you'll spend time absorbing data that doesn't matter, and you'll be tempted by the first choice that describes it correctly. Start with the claim; let it tell you where to look.
:::

## Reading graphics carefully

- **Check units and scales**: percent vs. number, thousands vs. millions, an axis that doesn't start at zero.
- **Match the legend**: in a grouped bar chart, make sure you're reading the right color or pattern.
- **Read the right comparison**: within one group across years, or across groups in one year?
- **"Highest/lowest" claims**: confirm against *every* data point, not just the neighbors.

## Practice

Use this table for the first two questions.

| Bike-share program | Trips per day, 2019 | Trips per day, 2023 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Riverton | 4,200 | 4,500 |
| Lakeside | 1,100 | 3,900 |
| Hill City | 2,800 | 2,600 |
| Bayport | 6,000 | 6,300 |

:::check
"A transportation researcher argues that adding protected bike lanes can dramatically increase bike-share use. Of the four cities in the table, only Lakeside built an extensive network of protected lanes between 2019 and 2023." Which choice best uses data from the table to support the researcher's argument?
- [ ] Bayport had the most trips per day in both 2019 and 2023.
- [x] Lakeside's daily trips more than tripled between 2019 and 2023, a far larger increase than in any other city.
- [ ] Hill City's daily trips declined slightly between 2019 and 2023.
- [ ] Riverton's daily trips rose by about 300 between 2019 and 2023.
> The argument is about a *dramatic increase*. All four choices describe the table accurately, but only B shows a dramatic change (1,100 → 3,900). A is about size, not change; C and D show small changes.
:::

:::check
Based on the table, which statement is accurate?
- [ ] Every program had more trips per day in 2023 than in 2019.
- [ ] Riverton had the largest increase in trips per day.
- [x] Hill City was the only program whose trips per day decreased.
- [ ] Lakeside had more trips per day than Riverton in 2023.
> Hill City went from 2,800 to 2,600; every other program rose. A is contradicted by Hill City, B by Lakeside (+2,800 vs. Riverton's +300), and D by the numbers (3,900 < 4,500).
:::

:::check
"In a study of sleep and learning, participants learned a list of words and were tested 24 hours later. Researchers concluded that a short nap after learning improved recall more than an equal period of quiet rest did." A graph shows average words recalled: Nap group 18, Quiet rest group 13, Video-watching group 12. Which choice best uses the data to support the researchers' conclusion?
- [ ] The video-watching group recalled the fewest words.
- [ ] The quiet rest and video-watching groups recalled similar numbers of words.
- [x] Participants who napped recalled an average of 18 words, compared with 13 for those who rested quietly.
- [ ] All three groups recalled more than 10 words on average.
> The conclusion compares napping with *quiet rest*. Only C compares exactly those two groups. The other choices are accurate but compare the wrong groups or none at all.
:::

## Summary

- The claim, usually in the last sentence, decides the answer; the graph confirms it.
- List the conditions the data must show, then eliminate.
- Several choices may be accurate; only one supports the specific claim.
- Read titles, units, and legends carefully when you do check the graphic.
