You don't need to memorize thousands of words for the digital SAT. Most vocabulary questions are solved with context clues. But two groups of words come up again and again, and knowing them saves time: **common words with a second meaning**, and the **academic vocabulary** that science and social-science passages use to talk about research.

The definitions below are short working definitions, with an example of each word used the way the SAT uses it.

## Common words, uncommon meanings

Meaning-in-context questions love these. The everyday meaning is the trap.

| Word | Second meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| afford | to provide, give | The balcony *affords* a view of the harbor. |
| appreciate | to recognize the value of; to increase in value | Land near the new station *appreciated* quickly. |
| arrest | to stop | The drug may *arrest* the growth of the tumor. |
| assume | to take on (a role or responsibility) | She *assumed* the role of director in 1998. |
| check | to restrain, limit | Predators *check* the rabbit population. |
| coin | to invent (a word or phrase) | The term was *coined* by a geologist. |
| compromise | to weaken, endanger | A single error could *compromise* the whole study. |
| conviction | strong belief | She spoke with *conviction*. |
| economy | careful, efficient use | The poet writes with great *economy*: no word is wasted. |
| exploit | to make full use of (not always negative) | The plant *exploits* gaps in the forest canopy. |
| facility | ease, natural skill | He had a *facility* for languages. |
| grave | serious | The report raised *grave* concerns. |
| harbor | to hold (a feeling or idea) | Some scientists *harbor* doubts about the theory. |
| novel | new, original | a *novel* approach to an old problem |
| qualify | to limit or add conditions to a statement | She *qualified* her praise: the plan was good, but only in the short term. |
| realize | to achieve, bring about | The city finally *realized* its plan for a waterfront park. |
| reserve | to hold back (judgment) | The panel *reserved* judgment until all the data were in. |
| sound | valid, reliable | a *sound* argument |
| static | unchanging | Language is never *static*. |
| table / shelve | to set aside, postpone | The council *tabled* the vote. |
| uniform | constant, the same throughout | The temperature was *uniform* across the tank. |
| upset | to disrupt (an expected outcome) | The new data *upset* the accepted timeline. |

## Words for research and argument

Science and social-science passages describe how claims are made, supported, and challenged. These words appear constantly in questions and answer choices.

| Making a claim | Supporting a claim | Challenging a claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **posit** – put forward as true | **bolster** – strengthen | **refute** – prove wrong |
| **hypothesize** – propose as an explanation to test | **substantiate** – give evidence for | **rebut** – argue against |
| **advance** (a theory) – propose | **corroborate** – confirm with more evidence | **undermine** – weaken |
| **speculate** – guess without firm evidence | **buttress** – reinforce | **skepticism** – doubt |

Other research terms:
- **empirical** – based on observation or experiment
- **replicate** – repeat a study and get the same results
- **control group** – the group in an experiment that doesn't receive the treatment
- **correlation** – two things varying together (not necessarily cause and effect)
- **anomaly** – something that doesn't fit the pattern
- **phenomenon** – an observable event or occurrence
- **indigenous** – native to a place
- **predisposed** – having a tendency toward
- **mitigate** – make less severe
- **inhibit / impede** – hold back, get in the way of
- **innate / inherent / intrinsic** – built in, natural
- **benign / innocuous** – harmless
- **salient / conspicuous** – noticeable, standing out
- **disparate / heterogeneous** – very different from each other
- **paucity / dearth** – a shortage; **plethora / profusion** – a large amount
- **contingent (on)** – dependent (on)
- **preliminary** – coming before the main part; early
- **prevailing / prevalent** – widespread, generally accepted

## Words that look negative but aren't

Some words begin with *in-*, *un-*, or sound critical but are actually neutral or positive. Don't eliminate them in a positive/negative sort.

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **critic** | someone who evaluates (positively or negatively) |
| **discerning** | perceptive, good at judging |
| **ineffable** | too great to be described in words |
| **infallible** | never wrong |
| **invaluable** | extremely valuable |
| **inimitable** | impossible to copy, unique |
| **unassuming** | modest |
| **unqualified** | complete, absolute ("an unqualified success") |

:::check
"The museum's new acoustics *afford* visitors the chance to hear the organ as its builders intended." As used in the text, what does "afford" most nearly mean?
- [ ] pay for
- [x] give
- [ ] risk
- [ ] demand
> The acoustics give visitors a chance. "Pay for" is the everyday meaning of "afford" and the trap.
:::

:::check
"Initial results seemed to *bolster* the hypothesis, but larger trials later found no effect." As used in the text, what does "bolster" most nearly mean?
- [ ] disprove
- [x] support
- [ ] replace
- [ ] simplify
> "But… later found no effect" contrasts with the initial results, which must have supported the hypothesis.
:::

:::check
"The documentary was an *unqualified* success: it won every major award and drew record audiences." As used in the text, what does "unqualified" most nearly mean?
- [ ] amateur
- [ ] undeserved
- [x] complete
- [ ] temporary
> The colon explains: every award, record audiences. "Unqualified" here means without any limitation, complete. "Amateur" relies on the more common meaning ("not qualified").
:::
