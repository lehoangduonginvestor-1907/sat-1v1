Words in Context questions open every Reading and Writing module. They come in two forms:

- **Sentence completion**: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?" There's a blank, and the choices are four words.
- **Meaning in context**: "As used in the text, what does the word *X* most nearly mean?" A word is underlined, and the choices are possible definitions.

Both test the same skill: using the surrounding sentence to figure out what meaning fits.

## Sentence completions

The passage always contains clues that point to the meaning of the missing word. Your job is to find them before you look at the choices.

:::steps
1. Read the whole passage, not just the sentence with the blank.
2. Find the **clue words**: the parts of the text that describe or explain the blank.
3. If you can, **predict** your own word for the blank. It doesn't need to be fancy; "good," "rare," or "made worse" is enough.
4. Decide whether the missing word is **positive, negative, or neutral**, and cross out choices with the wrong charge.
5. Plug the survivors back into the sentence and pick the one that means what the passage needs.
:::

### Types of clues

| Clue type | Signals | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Same direction** | *and, also, likewise, moreover, not only… but also, just as* | The blank matches nearby key words. |
| **Explanation** | *because, since, therefore, thus,* a colon, a dash, "in that" | The blank is explained by (or explains) the next part. |
| **Opposite direction** | *although, but, however, despite, unlike, whereas, while, yet,* "for all" (= despite) | The blank means the opposite of nearby key words. |

:::examples
✓ The coach was known for her ____ manner; she rarely raised her voice, even after a loss. → **calm** // The explanation after the semicolon defines the blank.
✓ Although the first reviews of the play were ____, audiences filled the theater for months. → **harsh** // "Although" signals the opposite of the audience's enthusiasm.
:::

### Positive / negative
Even when you can't predict an exact word, you can usually tell whether it's positive or negative. That alone can eliminate two choices.

Watch out for **double negatives**: "Researchers were relieved to find that the new pesticide is far less ____ to bees than older products: hives near treated fields stayed healthy." The overall message is positive, but because of *less*, the blank itself needs a **negative** word: "less *toxic*." A positive word like "beneficial" would flip the meaning.

### Choose the word that *means* the idea
Several choices may be compatible with the sentence; only one actually expresses the idea the clues demand. If the passage says a species is found in only one valley on Earth, the blank should mean "unique to that place" (*endemic*), not just something loosely related (*abundant*, *distinctive*).

:::trap Don't pick the hardest word
The test often uses difficult-looking words as wrong answers and a simple word as the right one. Never choose a word because it sounds sophisticated, and don't get stuck on words you don't know. Deal with every word you *do* know first; often you can eliminate three choices without knowing the fourth.
:::

## Meaning in context

These questions usually test a **second meaning** of a common word. The obvious, everyday meaning is the classic trap.

:::steps
1. Cover the underlined word and reread the sentence.
2. Predict a word that would fit in its place (read the sentence before and after if needed).
3. Find the choice that matches your prediction.
4. Plug it in: the sentence should still make sense.
:::

:::examples
✓ "The committee decided to *table* the proposal until the spring meeting." → postpone // Not a piece of furniture.
✓ "Her argument was *sound*: every step followed from the evidence." → valid // Not a noise.
✓ "The new policy may *check* the spread of the invasive plant." → restrain // Not "verify."
:::

If a choice gives the word's usual meaning, be suspicious. If one choice looks like an odd fit at first (*fluid* boundaries, *arresting* growth), it's probably being used in a less common sense, and that sense is what the question is testing.

:::check
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word? The documentary's narrator speaks in a deliberately ____ style, avoiding dramatic music and emotional language so that viewers can judge the evidence for themselves.
- [ ] passionate
- [x] restrained
- [ ] humorous
- [ ] confusing
> The clue is the explanation that follows: no dramatic music, no emotional language. That describes a restrained, understated style. "Passionate" is the opposite.
:::

:::check
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word? Despite the ____ of reliable records from the period, historians have reconstructed a surprisingly detailed picture of daily life in the village.
- [ ] abundance
- [ ] accuracy
- [x] scarcity
- [ ] publication
> "Despite" signals contrast with "a surprisingly detailed picture." Reconstructing a detailed picture is surprising only if records were scarce. "Abundance" reverses the logic.
:::

:::check
"The success of the expedition was *contingent* on the weather: a single week of storms would force the team to turn back." As used in the text, what does "contingent" most nearly mean?
- [ ] accidental
- [ ] temporary
- [x] dependent
- [ ] guaranteed
> The colon explains: storms could end the expedition, so its success depended on the weather. "Contingent on" means "dependent on."
:::

## Summary

- Find the clue words; predict; play positive/negative; plug in.
- Same-direction, explanation, and opposite-direction clues point to the meaning.
- Meaning-in-context questions usually test a second meaning. The everyday meaning is the trap.
- Never choose a word just because it looks difficult.
