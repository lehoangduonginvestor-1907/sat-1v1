The Reading questions on the digital SAT are short: each one comes with its own passage of roughly 25 to 150 words, and a few come as a pair of short texts. They make up the first part of every Reading and Writing module, before the grammar and transition questions. This lesson explains what you'll see and lays out a general method; the lessons that follow zoom in on each question type.

## What the passages look like

Passages come from four broad areas:
- **Literature**: excerpts from novels, short stories, plays, and poems, often from the 1800s or early 1900s.
- **Humanities**: art, music, literature, and history of ideas.
- **Social science**: economics, psychology, sociology, history.
- **Natural science**: biology, astronomy, earth science, chemistry. Some include a table or graph.

You never need outside knowledge. Everything you need is on the screen, and answers that rely on what you "know" about the topic are usually wrong.

## The order of question types in a module

Within the Reading portion, questions tend to appear in a fixed order by type: Words in Context first, then Text Structure and Purpose, Cross-Text Connections, Central Ideas and Details, Command of Evidence (textual and quantitative), and Inferences. Difficulty varies within each type, so a hard vocabulary question can come right before an easy main-idea question. If a question is eating your time, guess, mark it for review, and move on.

## Reading rhetorically

On the SAT you're not reading for enjoyment or for every detail. You're reading to understand **the argument and the role of each piece of information**: What is the author's main point? Which sentence is the claim, which is evidence, which is a counterargument? Where does the passage shift?

Pay extra attention to:
- **the first and last sentences**, where main points usually live;
- **transitions**, especially *however*, *but*, *yet*, *in fact*, *therefore*;
- words that announce importance: *key*, *crucial*, *significant*, *the point is*, *the goal*;
- **punctuation that signals explanation**: colons and dashes;
- **strong language**: *only*, *never*, *must*, *surprisingly*.

## How wrong answers are built

The test writers construct wrong answers in predictable ways. Learning the patterns makes eliminating them much faster.

| Wrong-answer type | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Off-topic** | Discusses something the passage never addresses. |
| **Too broad / wrong scope** | The passage is about one artist; the answer talks about "artists." |
| **Too extreme** | *always*, *never*, *completely*, *proves*, where the passage is more careful. |
| **Half right, half wrong** | The first half matches the passage, the second half adds or twists something. |
| **Could be true, but not supported** | Plausible in real life, but the passage doesn't say it. |
| **Right detail, wrong question** | Accurately restates a sentence, but not the part of the passage the question asks about. |
| **Reversed** | Gets the relationship backwards (cause/effect, who believes what). |

The last two choices you're deciding between are very often a "could be true" answer versus the real answer, or a "half-right" answer versus the real answer.

## Same idea, different words

Correct answers almost never copy the passage. They **paraphrase** it, usually in more general language. If the passage says researchers "found that the birds returned to the same nest every spring," the correct answer might say the study "documented a pattern of seasonal return." Choices that repeat the passage's exact phrasing are often traps that attach the familiar words to the wrong idea.

## A method for every question

:::steps
1. **Read the question first, and slowly.** Know whether it asks for a main idea, a function, a detail, a word, or evidence.
2. **Read the passage** with that job in mind. For main idea and purpose questions, focus on the first and last sentences; for a specific detail, find the exact lines.
3. **Answer in your own words** before looking at the choices. Even a three-word prediction protects you from attractive wrong answers.
4. **Go through the choices in order** and eliminate the ones that are clearly wrong. Keep anything you're unsure about.
5. **Down to two?** Go back to the passage. Find the specific word or phrase in each choice that is or isn't supported. One wrong word makes a whole answer wrong.
:::

:::trap Read whole sentences
Never start reading in the middle of a sentence or stop halfway through one. The key information is often in the half of the sentence you skipped: a word like *although* or *only* at the start can reverse what the rest seems to say.
:::

:::tip Be literal
Most mistakes on SAT Reading come from reading too much into a passage, not too little. If an answer requires you to assume something the text doesn't say, it's wrong, however reasonable the assumption sounds.
:::

:::check
A passage says: "Although the painter is best known today for her portraits, she spent the last decade of her career working almost exclusively on landscapes." Which answer is best supported?
- [ ] The painter's landscapes are more admired than her portraits.
- [ ] The painter abandoned portraits because they were unpopular.
- [x] The painter's later work focused on a different subject from the one she is now most associated with.
- [ ] The painter produced more landscapes than portraits over her career.
> C restates the sentence in general terms. A reverses the reputation, B invents a reason, and D makes a numerical claim the passage never makes ("a decade" says nothing about total numbers).
:::

:::check
A passage about a single species of desert beetle explains how it collects water from fog. A choice says: "Desert insects have developed many strategies for surviving drought." What's wrong with this choice as a main idea?
- [ ] It is too narrow.
- [x] It is too broad.
- [ ] It contradicts the passage.
- [ ] It is too negative.
> The passage is about one beetle and one strategy. "Desert insects" and "many strategies" go far beyond that scope.
:::
