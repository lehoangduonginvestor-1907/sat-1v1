Cross-Text Connections questions give you two short passages, **Text 1** and **Text 2**, on the same topic, and ask how they relate: "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim in Text 1?" There's usually one such question per module. The passages are short, but juggling two arguments at once makes it easy to mix them up.

## The usual relationship: disagreement

Most often, the two texts **disagree**: Text 1 presents a view or a finding, and Text 2 challenges it, qualifies it, or offers a different explanation. But they can also **partly agree**: Text 2 might accept Text 1's finding while warning about a limitation. Correct answers can involve both agreement and disagreement, so read carefully.

## The method

:::steps
1. **Read Text 1** and write its main point in a few words.
2. **Read Text 2** and write its main point.
3. **Write the relationship**: disagree completely? agree on X but not Y? Text 2 adds a complication?
4. **Answer the question in your own words** ("Text 2's author would say Text 1 overestimates the effect").
5. **Choose** the option closest to your answer.
:::

Doing the work up front matters. Once you know the relationship, you've often answered the question before you read the choices.

:::examples
✓ Text 1: A new study shows that a four-day school week improves attendance and teacher retention. Text 2: An economist notes that in districts with four-day weeks, students' test scores in math fell slightly. → Relationship: Text 2 accepts some benefits may exist but points out a cost Text 1 ignores. // Likely answer: "It overlooks a possible drawback."
:::

## Using the relationship to eliminate

- If the authors **disagree**, eliminate positive and neutral choices ("It is strongly supported by…," "It is consistent with…"). The first few words often reveal a choice's attitude.
- Avoid **extreme** choices ("can *only* be applied…," "is *entirely* false") unless the passage is just as extreme.
- **Check attribution.** A favorite trap takes a detail from Text 1 and presents it as Text 2's view (or vice versa). Before choosing, find the detail in the text the choice says it comes from.

:::tip Typical correct-answer shapes
"It overstates the effect." · "It ignores an important factor." · "It is plausible, but the evidence doesn't support it." · "It is accurate in some cases but not others." · "It acknowledges a benefit but cautions against excess." · "Text 2 offers an alternative explanation."
:::

:::check
Text 1: "Many historians credit the printing press with rapidly spreading literacy in fifteenth-century Europe. Printed books were far cheaper than handwritten manuscripts, so more people could afford them." Text 2: "Literacy rates in many regions changed little for nearly a century after printing arrived. Books may have become cheaper, but most people still lacked schools and the time to learn to read." Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the historians in Text 1?
- [ ] By agreeing that cheaper books immediately produced widespread literacy.
- [x] By arguing that lower book prices alone did not quickly raise literacy.
- [ ] By denying that printed books were cheaper than manuscripts.
- [ ] By claiming that handwritten manuscripts were more widely read than printed books.
> Text 2 accepts that books "may have become cheaper" but says literacy changed little because of other barriers. B captures that. C contradicts Text 2's concession; D isn't in either text.
:::

:::check
Text 1: "Urban gardens are often promoted as a way to reduce food insecurity. A survey of community gardeners found that most of them ate more vegetables after joining a garden." Text 2: "Gardens clearly benefit participants, but those participants tend to be people who already have spare time and stable housing. The residents facing the greatest food insecurity rarely join." Which choice best describes how Text 2 relates to Text 1?
- [ ] Text 2 rejects the survey's finding that gardeners ate more vegetables.
- [x] Text 2 accepts that gardens help participants but questions whether they reach those most in need.
- [ ] Text 2 argues that urban gardens should be replaced by grocery stores.
- [ ] Text 2 provides additional evidence that gardens eliminate food insecurity.
> Text 2 agrees gardens "clearly benefit participants" (partial agreement) but notes the most food-insecure residents rarely join. A contradicts that agreement; C and D aren't supported.
:::

## Summary

- Main point of Text 1 → main point of Text 2 → relationship → your own answer → choose.
- Disagreement is most common, but watch for partial agreement.
- Eliminate choices with the wrong attitude, extreme wording, or details attributed to the wrong text.
