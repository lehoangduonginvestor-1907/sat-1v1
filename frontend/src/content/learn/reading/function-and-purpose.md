Text Structure and Purpose questions ask **why** something is in the passage: "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?" or "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?" A common experience: you understand the lines perfectly, then none of the answers seems to describe them. That happens because these questions aren't asking *what* the lines say. They're asking *what job* the lines do.

## Content vs. function

You need the content to find the function, but content alone isn't enough.

| Content (what it says) | Function (why it's there) |
| --- | --- |
| "Sea otters crack open shellfish with rocks they carry in a pouch of skin." | *to give an example* of tool use in marine animals |
| "Some critics, however, argue that the method is too expensive to scale." | *to introduce a counterargument* |
| "At least, that is what the records suggest." | *to qualify* (limit) the previous claim |

Answer choices use abstract verbs (*illustrate, emphasize, qualify, introduce, contrast, explain*), so you have to connect the specific words in the passage to these general descriptions.

## Where the answer comes from

Function questions fall into two groups, and you can't tell in advance which one you're facing:

1. **The answer is inside the lines**: a word like *but*, *in short*, *for example*, or a colon within the underlined sentence tells you its job.
2. **The answer is in the context**: the underlined sentence supports a point made **before** it (or, less often, after it).

:::steps
1. Read the underlined portion, then read the sentence before it (and after it, if needed). If the underlined part is near the start, read from the beginning.
2. Ask: **what point does this sentence support or respond to?** Supporting details usually sit in the middle of a passage; the points they support sit at the beginning or end.
3. Describe the function in your own words ("gives an example of the claim in sentence 1," "shows a contrast with the previous idea").
4. Match it to the choice with the same job.
:::

## Signal words and what they do

| Function | Signals |
| --- | --- |
| continue / add | also, moreover, furthermore, in addition |
| contrast | but, however, yet, whereas, although, in contrast |
| cause / result | because, therefore, thus, as a result, consequently |
| example / illustrate | for example, for instance, such as |
| explain / define | a colon, a dash, that is, in other words |
| emphasize | indeed, in fact, italics, repetition, *crucial, central, key* |
| speculate | may, might, could, perhaps, possibly |
| compare | like, just as, similarly, likewise |
| qualify (limit a claim) | parentheses or dashes with an aside, "at least," "in some cases," "if the account is accurate" |

## Main point vs. primary purpose

For "main purpose of the text" questions, don't just restate the main point. The **point** is the claim; the **purpose** is the rhetorical goal: to describe, to explain, to argue, to present a finding, to challenge a view.

If a passage's point is "a self-taught engineer designed the first reliable water pump for the village," a purpose answer might be "to describe how a particular invention came about," which uses more general words than the passage. The first sentence often reveals the purpose; skim the rest to confirm.

## Positive, negative, neutral

Play positive/negative with the answer choices. An enthusiastic passage won't have a function like "criticize" or "lament"; a neutral science passage won't "celebrate" or "mock."

:::trap Extreme verbs are usually wrong
Choices with strong verbs such as *prove, disprove, discredit, condemn, mock, ridicule, dismiss* are rarely correct. Research passages describe evidence and theories; they almost never settle a question for good. Moderate verbs (*describe, suggest, illustrate, qualify, present*) are much more likely.
:::

:::tip Second meanings again
If you see *qualify a claim* or a *novel* idea (meaning "new") among the choices, take a careful look. The test often rewards these less common meanings. Still confirm against the passage.
:::

:::check
"Many people assume that honeybees are the most important pollinators of crops. [[In fact, a study of 40 crops worldwide found that wild insects, such as solitary bees and hoverflies, were twice as effective at producing fruit as managed honeybees.]] The findings suggest that protecting wild habitats near farms could raise yields." Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence?
- [ ] It offers an example of how honeybees are managed on farms.
- [x] It presents evidence that challenges a common assumption.
- [ ] It explains why wild habitats are disappearing.
- [ ] It describes a disagreement among researchers.
> The first sentence gives the assumption ("many people assume…"); the underlined sentence begins with "In fact" and gives a study result that contradicts it. That's evidence challenging an assumption.
:::

:::check
"The novelist claimed to have written the entire book in six weeks [[(though her letters from that year mention revisions that lasted well into the following spring)]]." Which choice best describes the function of the parenthetical portion?
- [ ] To praise the novelist's speed.
- [x] To cast doubt on the novelist's account.
- [ ] To explain why the novel was successful.
- [ ] To describe the plot of the novel.
> The parenthesis adds evidence that the six-week claim may not be accurate. In other words, it qualifies or casts doubt on the claim.
:::

:::check
"Engineers designing bridges in earthquake zones once focused on making structures as rigid as possible. Today, many designs do the opposite: they include flexible joints and sliding bearings that let the bridge move with the ground instead of resisting it." Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?
- [ ] To argue that rigid bridges should be demolished.
- [ ] To explain how earthquakes are measured.
- [x] To describe a shift in how engineers approach earthquake-resistant bridges.
- [ ] To criticize engineers who designed older bridges.
> "Once… Today… the opposite" = a shift in approach. A and D are too extreme and negative; B is off-topic.
:::

## Summary

- Function = why the lines are there, not what they say.
- Read before (and after) the lines; find the point they support.
- Use signal words to identify the job: contrast, example, explanation, qualification…
- Purpose questions use general rhetorical verbs; extreme verbs are usually wrong.
